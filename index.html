Foundations

Object relationships

Before the patterns make sense, the lines in the diagrams have to. In Java, association, aggregation and composition all look the same: A holds a reference to B. What separates them is what each one claims about ownership — who created the part, and what happens to it when the whole is deleted.

R1

Association

knows about

One object keeps a link to another, but neither one owns the other.

What it is

This is the weakest lasting link. A keeps a reference to B — a field, or a list of them — so it can call B. B was made somewhere else, may be used by others, and keeps living when A is gone. The numbers on the line (1, 0..1, 0..*) and the direction of the arrow are part of what you are saying.

Lifetime and ownership

Nobody owns anybody here. So deleting one side must never delete the other. The direction is a real choice: a one-way link means only one side can reach the other, and that is usually what you want. Two-way links cause most of the bugs, because both ends have to be kept in step. One side must be made responsible for that, or the two ends will disagree with each other.

Model it this way when

  • Both objects have their own identity and their own life.
  • The link lasts longer than one method call, so it is worth keeping in a field.
  • The object being linked to may be used by several others.

Examples

  • An Order that links to the Customer who placed it.
  • A Student and the Courses they joined, where both exist on their own.
  • Any helper object passed in through the constructor.
  • A domain object that holds the id of another one.

Watch out for

  • Drawing a line for something that is only a method argument. That is a dependency.
  • A two-way link with nobody responsible for keeping both ends correct.
  • Loops that later break JSON output, equals, hashCode or garbage collection.

Compare with

Aggregation and composition are the same link plus a claim about ownership. A dependency is weaker than all three: it lasts only while the method runs.

R2

Aggregation

has-a, shared

A whole holds parts that it does not own. The parts can live alone and be shared.

What it is

Drawn as a hollow diamond on the whole. The container keeps parts together for some purpose, but each part was made somewhere else, may belong to another whole later, and keeps living after the container is gone. In short: has one, but did not make it and will not destroy it.

Lifetime and ownership

The difference from composition answers one question: what happens when you delete. If closing a team must not delete the players, that is aggregation, and the drawing should say so. In plain Java the two look the same, because both are just fields, so the diamond is a note about intent. It becomes real at the database layer, where it decides your cascade and orphan-removal settings.

Model it this way when

  • The parts are useful on their own, outside the whole.
  • Parts can be added, removed, or moved to another whole while the program runs.
  • The same part may belong to more than one whole.

Examples

  • A Team that holds Players who stay players after the team closes.
  • A Playlist that holds Tracks which other playlists also use.
  • A Department that holds Employees who can move to another one.
  • A cart that holds links to products in the catalogue.

Watch out for

  • Using it when the part means nothing without the whole. That is composition.
  • Letting the diamond suggest a rule that no code and no database constraint checks.
  • Spending time on the difference when nothing in your system depends on it.

Compare with

It sits between association and composition. If the part cannot live alone, make it composition. If you are claiming no ownership at all, it is just an association.

R3

Composition

owns outright

The whole owns its parts. They are made with it and deleted with it.

What it is

Drawn as a filled diamond on the whole. This is a strong “has a”. The part is usually created inside the owner, is never shared with another whole, and means nothing on its own. Delete an invoice and its lines go too, because one line by itself is not useful to anyone.

Lifetime and ownership

This is the strongest thing you can say about lifetime, and it gives you something back: because nothing else holds the part, the whole can keep itself correct. But that only works if the part never escapes. A getter that returns the real list quietly turns composition into aggregation, because now an outsider holds a part you said you owned, and can change it behind your back.

Model it this way when

  • The part is created inside the whole and never handed out for editing.
  • The part cannot be shared with, or moved to, another whole.
  • Deleting the whole must delete the parts.

Examples

  • An Order and its OrderLines.
  • An Invoice and its lines; a House and its Rooms.
  • A DDD aggregate root and everything inside its boundary.
  • A Composite node and its children.

Watch out for

  • Returning the internal list directly. Return a copy or a read-only view.
  • Claiming composition while another object also holds the part.
  • Saying the whole owns something that the business clearly moves elsewhere.

Compare with

Aggregation has the same shape but no ownership. Inheritance answers a different question: composition says has a, inheritance says is a, and the two are alternatives far more often than people expect.

Foundations

SOLID principles

Five principles that describe what good structure is trying to achieve. The patterns are the solutions; SOLID is the standard you judge them by. That is why the honest answer to “why use this pattern?” is nearly always one of these five. Each one below also says what it costs when you take it too far.

S

Single Responsibility

one reason to change

A class should have one reason to change.

What it says

This is not “a class should do one thing”, which is too vague to help anyone. The useful version is about change. If the finance team, the design team and the DBA can each ask for an edit to the same class, then it has three jobs. It will be changed three times as often, by three people who do not know what the others need.

Why it matters

Reasons to change are what cause merge conflicts, bugs, and fear of opening a file. When you split a class by who asks for changes, a request lands in one place and you can see what it affects. The cost is splitting too far. Do that and one small feature touches six files, each too small to make sense on its own. Split when a second reason really exists, not when you think one might appear.

It applies when

  • The history of one file shows edits made for completely different reasons.
  • To test one behaviour, you must build or fake three others.
  • The class name needs an “and”, or has given up and become Manager, Helper or Util.

What it looks like applied

  • Taking calculation, formatting and saving out of one report class.
  • Keeping controllers thin: HTTP in the controller, steps in the service, rules in the domain.
  • Pulling out a pricing rule that product managers change often from an order class they never touch.

Common misreadings

  • Reading it as “one method per class”.
  • Splitting only by technical layer, so every business change still edits five files.
  • Using it to justify classes that hold data and no behaviour at all.

How it connects

Interface Segregation is the same idea applied to interfaces instead of classes. Facade is what you build when splitting has left callers with too many pieces to put together.

O

Open–Closed

extend, don't edit

Add new behaviour by adding new code, not by editing code that already works.

What it says

Put an interface at the place where you expect change, and let each new version arrive as a new class. The code that works is never opened again. The new behaviour simply plugs in.

Why it matters

Every edit to working code is a chance to break it, and the risk is highest in the code that everything uses. The classic example is a switch that gains a case for every new type, in several methods at once. Here is the fair warning though: you have to guess where change will come from, and a wrong guess means redoing the interface later anyway. So use this on the second version, when you can see the pattern — not on the first, when you are still guessing.

It applies when

  • You are adding a third case to a conditional you have already edited twice.
  • A new version should be shippable as a new class or plugin.
  • Different teams or customers each need their own version of one step.

What it looks like applied

  • Payment methods, shipping rules and discounts behind one interface.
  • Validation rules registered as beans instead of listed in a long if chain.
  • Export formats added as new writer classes.
  • Plugins loaded when the app starts.

Common misreadings

  • Reading it as “never change code”. You change plenty. You just do not reopen the core for every variant.
  • Adding interfaces early for change that never arrives.
  • Extension points so general that nobody can tell what the code does.

How it connects

Most creational and behavioural patterns exist to make this possible. A Liskov problem usually causes an Open–Closed problem right behind it, because callers start adding type checks.

L

Liskov Substitution

substitutable

Any subclass must work wherever the parent type is expected, without the caller noticing.

What it says

This is about behaviour, not just method names, so the compiler cannot check it for you. A subclass must not ask for more than the parent asked for, promise less than the parent promised, break the parent's rules, or throw errors that callers were never warned about. If code has to check which subclass it holds before it is safe, the rule is already broken.

Why it matters

The whole point of polymorphism is that callers are written once, against the parent type. When a subclass quietly changes the deal, that benefit flips into a cost: every caller adds defensive checks, and those checks are exactly what Open–Closed was meant to prevent. The usual example is Square extends Rectangle. It is fine until a caller sets width and height separately and the area is not what the parent promised. The lesson is not that inheritance is bad. It is that “is a” in real life does not always mean “can replace” in code.

It applies when

  • Any time you extend a class or implement an interface.
  • Above all when an override throws, does nothing, or accepts less than the parent did.
  • When callers of the parent type start using instanceof.

What it looks like applied

  • Replacing an inheritance tree with composition when the promise cannot be kept.
  • Making the parent's promise smaller and honest, so every subclass can keep it.
  • Modelling shapes as read-only values with an area() method, which removes the problem completely.

Common misreadings

  • Assuming the compiler checks this. It checks method signatures, not behaviour.
  • Reading it as “never use inheritance”.
  • Writing a comment about the surprise instead of fixing the model.

How it connects

Interface Segregation often removes these problems at the source. A subclass forced to implement methods it cannot support is usually implementing an interface that was too big.

I

Interface Segregation

small roles

No class should be forced to depend on methods it never uses.

What it says

Prefer several small interfaces over one big one. The important part is who owns the interface: it belongs to the code that uses it, not to the class that implements it. So name it and size it for that user.

Why it matters

A big interface ties every user to every method on it. Implementations end up throwing “not supported” for half of them, tests fake twelve methods to use one, and a change made for one user forces a rebuild for everyone else. The opposite danger is going too far: split enough and a class needs five constructor arguments that all point at the same object. Split by the role someone plays, not by counting methods.

It applies when

  • Some implementations cannot really support parts of the interface.
  • Unrelated pieces of code share one interface for unrelated reasons.
  • Faking the interface in a test is far more work than what the test actually uses.

What it looks like applied

  • Splitting a repository into a read side and a write side.
  • One small interface per use case, instead of one big Service per entity.
  • Small function-style interfaces passed in as behaviour.
  • Read-only views given to code that must not change anything.

Common misreadings

  • Making every interface single-method out of habit.
  • Splitting so far that a caller must collect five objects to do one job.
  • One interface per class. That is naming, not splitting.

How it connects

This is Single Responsibility applied to the contract instead of the class, and it is often the cheapest fix for a Liskov problem.

D

Dependency Inversion

depend on abstractions

Business rules should not depend on details. Both should depend on an interface — and the rules should own it.

What it says

There are two halves here, and the second is the one people forget. First, depend on an interface instead of a concrete class. Second, that interface is written by the business code, in the words the business uses. The detail class then implements it. So the code dependency now points from the detail back towards the rules, which is the opposite of the way the calls travel. That flip is the inversion.

Why it matters

It keeps the part that holds your business decisions free from things that change for outside reasons: frameworks, vendors, databases, message formats. That freedom is what lets you test the rules without infrastructure and replace a vendor without a rewrite. At module level this is exactly what hexagonal, or ports and adapters, means. The cost is extra ceremony if you do it everywhere: an interface with one permanent implementation and no real boundary under it buys you nothing.

It applies when

  • There is a real boundary: a database, an outside API, a message broker, even the clock.
  • The thing you depend on changes for reasons that have nothing to do with your business.
  • You want to test business rules without starting any infrastructure.

What it looks like applied

  • Writing OrderRepository in the domain and implementing it in the persistence module.
  • A NotificationPort in the core, with email and SMS adapters outside it.
  • Passing in a Clock, so rules that depend on time can be tested.
  • Constructor injection everywhere, so dependencies are visible in the signature.

Common misreadings

  • Thinking it means “use a DI framework”. The container is a convenience; the inversion is a design choice.
  • Putting the interface next to its only implementation in the infrastructure package. Nothing was inverted.
  • Wrapping stable things. You do not need a StringPort.

How it connects

Dependency injection is the technique, dependency inversion is the principle, and an IoC container is the tool that does it for you. They are three different things, and telling them apart cleanly is a common senior interview question.

5 patterns

Creational

Creational patterns take the new keyword out of the code that uses the object. They answer three questions. Who decides which class to build? When is it built? And how do you put a complicated one together? Get this wrong and every new type means editing code that already works.

01

Singleton

one instance

Make sure a class has only one object, and give everyone one way to reach it.

What it is

The class hides its constructor, so nobody outside can create it. It hands out the one object through a static method. The class builds that object itself, usually the first time somebody asks for it.

Why it exists

A global variable also gives you one object. But then nothing controls when it is built, or whether it is built correctly. A Singleton keeps that control inside the class that owns it. The downside is real though. The object is global. Classes that use it do not show it in their constructor, so the dependency is hidden. And tests start affecting each other. In Java you usually want the benefit without the pain, and a DI container gives you exactly that: one object per application, passed in openly.

Use it when

  • Only one object should manage a shared thing, such as a pool of database connections.
  • Many unrelated places need it, and passing it through every constructor is painful.
  • Building it is slow, and the result is safe to share between threads.

Typical use cases

  • Configuration read once at startup and used everywhere.
  • A connection pool or thread pool that must not be created twice.
  • An id generator that must never hand out the same value twice.
  • A logging or metrics entry point in front of a global registry.

Think twice when

  • You may later need one per customer, per region, or per test.
  • You are only using it to avoid passing an argument. That is a global variable in disguise.
  • The object holds data that several threads write to.

Don't confuse it with

A Spring bean with singleton scope means one object per container, not one per JVM, and Spring owns it. Your class stays an ordinary class with normal constructor arguments, so it is still easy to test. Being able to explain that difference is a common interview question.

02

Factory Method

subclass decides

Put object creation in a method that subclasses can override, so each subclass picks the class.

What it is

A base class has a method that returns some product type. The rest of the base class uses only that type, never a real class name. Each subclass overrides the method and returns the class it wants. So new lives in one place that can be changed.

Why it exists

Without this, the code around the object has to name a real class. Every new type then means editing that code, usually a switch that keeps growing. Here you add a subclass instead, and the working code is left alone. The cost is a second set of classes: one creator for each product. That is too much for two cases, where one static method or a map of suppliers is enough.

Use it when

  • The class cannot know in advance which type it must build.
  • The steps around the object stay the same, and only the object changes.
  • You are writing a framework, and the people using it supply the objects.

Typical use cases

  • A dialog framework where each platform subclass makes its own button.
  • An editor whose subclasses create the right kind of document.
  • Parsers or codecs chosen by which creator you are using.
  • Thread factories that set the name, priority and daemon flag on new threads.

Think twice when

  • One static method with a switch is genuinely enough. Do not grow a hierarchy for two cases.
  • The choice comes from data, not from a type. A Map<String, Supplier<T>> is simpler.

Don't confuse it with

Factory Method changes one product using inheritance. Abstract Factory changes a whole group using composition, and usually has several factory methods inside it. A static of() or valueOf() helper is a static factory method — handy, but not this pattern, because nothing overrides it.

03

Abstract Factory

product family

Create a group of matching objects without naming any of their real classes.

What it is

One interface with one create method per product. Each factory implements all of them and returns products that belong together. Your code holds the interface, so it can never mix a product from one group with a product from another.

Why it exists

The real problem this solves is keeping the group consistent, not creating objects. If a Postgres query builder must be used with a Postgres dialect and a Postgres type mapper, one factory turns that rule into code instead of a note on a wiki page. The cost is one-sided: adding a whole new group is easy, but adding a new product type changes the interface and every factory that implements it.

Use it when

  • The products must be used together, and mixing groups would be a bug.
  • The whole group is chosen once, at startup or from configuration.
  • Your code must not know any vendor class names.

Typical use cases

  • UI toolkits that produce a matching set of widgets per platform.
  • Storage: one factory gives the S3 client, uploader and URL signer; another gives the GCS ones.
  • Database-specific data access classes behind one interface.
  • Test doubles: one factory that returns a matching set of fakes.

Think twice when

  • You expect to keep adding product types. Each one touches every factory.
  • There is only one product. Use Factory Method or a builder instead.

Don't confuse it with

Each create method inside is a Factory Method, and the factories themselves are often singletons. Builder puts together one complicated object step by step. Abstract Factory hands you several finished objects that fit together.

04

Builder

step-by-step

Put a complex object together step by step, so the same steps can produce different results.

What it is

Instead of one long constructor, a builder collects values one named step at a time. The real object is created at the end, in build(). Because everything is known by then, the object can be checked once and can be read-only afterwards.

Why it exists

Constructors use position, not names. new Report(true, false, true, 30) is hard to read, and silently wrong if two flags swap places. Many optional values lead to many constructors. A builder gives you named steps, sensible defaults, one place for checks, and an object that cannot change later. The cost is one extra class, and records and generated builders make that cost small.

Use it when

  • An object has many optional values, or several values of the same type.
  • You want the object to be read-only but cannot supply everything at once.
  • Checking the values only makes sense once you have all of them.
  • The object is built up in steps, as with a parser or a query builder.

Typical use cases

  • HTTP request and client configuration objects.
  • SQL or criteria query builders.
  • Test data, where each test changes one field of a valid baseline.
  • The classic form: one Director drives a Builder to produce HTML, Markdown or PDF from the same input.

Think twice when

  • Three or four required values and nothing optional. A constructor or a record is clearer.
  • A half-filled builder can escape and be mistaken for the finished object.

Don't confuse it with

Builder is about how one object is put together. Abstract Factory is about which group of objects you get. A builder gives you the object at the end of a sequence; a factory gives it to you straight away.

05

Prototype

clone it

Make a new object by copying one you already have, instead of building it from scratch.

What it is

An object knows how to copy itself. You take one that is already set up, copy it, and change the few fields that differ. You skip all the setup work that was done the first time.

Why it exists

Often the slow part is not creating the object, but everything before it: reading a template, loading reference data, opening a connection. Copying skips that. The object is also its own factory, so you need no extra class hierarchy. The hard part is the copy itself. A shallow copy still shares the inner objects, a deep copy has to handle loops, and Java's Cloneable is known to be a poor design — so a copy constructor or a static copyOf is safer.

Use it when

  • Copying is measurably cheaper than building.
  • You need many objects that differ from one baseline in only a few fields.
  • The class to create is decided while the program runs, from a set of ready examples.
  • You need a snapshot of an object as it is right now.

Typical use cases

  • Document or report templates copied for each user.
  • Game characters created from a ready-made template.
  • Test data: one valid baseline object, copied and changed per test.
  • Undo checkpoints, where the copy is the saved state.

Think twice when

  • The object holds things you cannot copy, like an open socket or file.
  • The object graph is deep and has loops. Then the copy code becomes the bug.
  • The object never changes anyway. Then sharing it is already safe.

Don't confuse it with

Prototype provides the copying that Memento needs for snapshots, and a registry of ready examples is usually a singleton. Factory Method builds from a class; Prototype builds from an object you already have.

7 patterns

Structural

Structural patterns are about putting objects together into bigger structures without making them rigid. Most of them are wrappers, and the useful skill is telling them apart. Does the wrapper change the interface, add behaviour, control access, or hide a crowd of classes behind one door?

06

Adapter

translate

Turn one interface into the one your code expects, so two pieces can work together.

What it is

The adapter implements the interface you want, and holds the object you actually have. Every call is translated: names, arguments, units, error styles. Neither your code nor the other class has to change.

Why it exists

You rarely control the shape of an outside SDK, an old service, or generated code. Without an adapter, that vendor's classes and errors spread through your codebase, and switching vendor becomes a rewrite. With one, the mismatch stays inside a single small class that is easy to test and easy to replace. In domain-driven design the same idea at module level is called an anti-corruption layer.

Use it when

  • You want to reuse a class whose methods do not match what you need.
  • Several similar outside APIs must sit behind one interface of yours.
  • An old component must work with new code without being rewritten.

Typical use cases

  • Payment providers such as Stripe, Razorpay and PayPal behind one PaymentGateway.
  • Wrapping a blocking client so it fits a reactive interface.
  • Putting an old XML API behind a JSON service layer.
  • Making an old Enumeration usable where an Iterator is expected.

Think twice when

  • You control both sides. Fix the interface instead of covering it up.
  • The translation needs real business rules. Those belong in a service, not an adapter.

Don't confuse it with

All four wrapper patterns look the same on a whiteboard. Adapter changes the interface. Decorator keeps it and adds behaviour. Proxy keeps it and controls access. Facade adds a new, smaller interface in front of many classes.

07

Bridge

two axes

Split what something is from how it is done, so the two sides can change on their own.

What it is

Instead of one class tree carrying two kinds of change, you make two trees and join them with a field. The upper class holds an interface and passes the basic work to it, while adding its own higher-level behaviour on top.

Why it exists

When two things change at the same time — message type and channel, shape and renderer, report and export format — inheritance makes you write every pair. That is N × M classes, and each new item on one side multiplies everything on the other. Holding a field instead turns it into N + M. You can also swap the lower half while the program runs, and hide it from callers. The cost is one more layer, which is not worth paying if only one side ever really changes.

Use it when

  • Two things change at once, and you are writing a class for every combination.
  • The implementation must be chosen at runtime or per environment.
  • Platform or vendor details must stay hidden from callers.

Typical use cases

  • Notifications (alert, reminder, digest) sent over channels (email, SMS, push).
  • JDBC: one set of interfaces over many database drivers.
  • A logging API whose real logger is chosen at deploy time.
  • Shapes drawn by either a vector or a raster engine.

Think twice when

  • There is one implementation and no realistic second one.
  • The upper class only forwards calls and adds nothing of its own.

Don't confuse it with

Bridge and Adapter end up looking alike, but they come from opposite directions. You plan a Bridge from the start, so both halves can grow. You add an Adapter later, to join two things that already exist and cannot be changed.

08

Composite

part–whole tree

Build a tree of objects, and let calling code treat one object and a whole branch the same way.

What it is

One interface is used both by leaves, which do the real work, and by containers, which hold children. A container usually does its job by asking each child and adding up the answers. The recursion lives in the tree, not in the code that uses it.

Why it exists

Otherwise every caller needs a type check and two code paths, and those checks multiply with every new operation. With this pattern, root.size() works whether the root is one file or a folder ten levels deep. The price is that the shared interface has to fit both sides, so a leaf inherits add() and remove() that it cannot support. You trade a little type safety for one simple interface.

Use it when

  • The data really is a tree of wholes and parts.
  • Callers should not care whether they hold one item or a container.
  • The operations are naturally recursive: totals, drawing, validation, permissions.

Typical use cases

  • File systems, where files and folders both answer size() and delete().
  • UI trees, where a panel draws itself by drawing its children.
  • Org charts, category trees, and parts lists.
  • Expression trees, where both nodes and numbers can be evaluated.

Think twice when

  • The data is flat, or leaves and containers genuinely need different methods.
  • The tree can get deep enough to overflow the stack. Then use a loop instead of recursion.

Don't confuse it with

You usually walk a Composite with an Iterator, or add new operations to it with a Visitor. Its nodes are often wrapped by Decorators — which is why a Decorator is sometimes described as a Composite with exactly one child.

09

Decorator

wrap & add

Add extra behaviour to one object while the program runs, keeping the same interface.

What it is

A decorator implements the interface and also holds an object of that interface. It passes each call on and adds work before or after it. Because the decorator is the same type, decorators can be stacked in any order you like.

Why it exists

Using subclasses for optional features does not scale. Buffered, compressed, encrypted and logged would need a class for every combination. Decorators combine at runtime, in any order, and each concern stays in its own small class. The costs appear later: many small objects, long stack traces, and identity checks that stop working, because the wrapper is not the same object and may not be the type the caller expects.

Use it when

  • Behaviour must be added to one object, not to a whole class.
  • Features are optional and can be combined freely.
  • Extra concerns like caching, retries, metrics or rate limiting must stay out of the main class.
  • The class you want to extend is final or otherwise closed.

Typical use cases

  • Java's I/O streams: buffering, compression and encryption stacked over a raw stream.
  • A caching or retrying wrapper around a repository or HTTP client.
  • Read-only or thread-safe views of a collection.
  • Request and response wrappers in servlet filters.

Think twice when

  • The behaviour is not optional. Put it in the class.
  • Callers need the real type, or compare objects by reference.
  • Ten layers deep, nobody can work out where a failure came from.

Don't confuse it with

Decorator and Proxy have the same drawing. A decorator is handed the object and adds behaviour the caller asked for. A proxy usually finds or creates the object and controls access to it. Chain of Responsibility also stacks wrappers, but a handler there may stop the request, while a decorator always passes the call on.

Facade

one door

Give one simple entry point to a complicated group of classes.

What it is

A small class offers the few operations most callers need, and does all the fiddly calls inside. It does not lock the subsystem away: code that needs more can still go straight to it. It only makes the common path easy.

Why it exists

Complexity that every caller has to repeat is complexity multiplied. If ten places each have to open a connection, set the isolation level, prepare a statement, read the results and close everything in the right order, then there are ten places to get it wrong. A facade holds that sequence once, which also means the subsystem behind it can be changed freely. The failure mode is a facade that keeps growing until it is the whole application.

Use it when

  • A subsystem is complicated and most callers need only a small part of it.
  • You are separating layers and want a narrow join between them.
  • You want callers to be independent of a library you might replace.

Typical use cases

  • An application service that turns “place order” into inventory, payment, shipping and email calls.
  • Helper classes that hide a long resource-handling sequence.
  • One startup() that wires and starts a dozen components in the right order.
  • A small client SDK over a large internal API.

Think twice when

  • It turns into a bag of unrelated methods.
  • It just forwards one call to one class. Nothing got simpler.

Don't confuse it with

Facade wraps many objects in a new and simpler interface. Adapter wraps one object in an interface that already exists. Mediator also sits in the middle, but its parts talk back to it, while nothing inside a subsystem knows the facade exists.

Flyweight

share state

Share the identical parts of many similar objects, so a huge number of them fits in memory.

What it is

The state is split in two. Shared state is the same everywhere and never changes, so one object can serve everybody. The rest depends on the situation and is passed in as an argument each time. A factory keeps a pool and returns an existing object whenever it can.

Why it exists

This is a memory trick, and saving memory is the only good reason to use it. A million trees that differ only in position do not need a million copies of the same mesh and texture. Moving the shared part into a pool can cut memory use hugely, in return for pool lookups and code that is harder to read. Measure first. On the JVM, simply restructuring the data often solves the same problem with far less trouble.

Use it when

  • There are so many objects that memory, not speed, is the limit.
  • Most of each object is the same as the others and can be made read-only.
  • Callers do not care whether two of them are the same object.

Typical use cases

  • Letter shapes in a text editor, shared per character and drawn at different positions.
  • Terrain, sprite or particle types in a game engine.
  • Shared text and small boxed numbers held in a pool.
  • One shared column description used by millions of rows.

Think twice when

  • You have not measured yet. This trades clear code for bytes.
  • The shared part cannot be made read-only.
  • The extra arguments make every call site worse than the memory you saved.

Don't confuse it with

The pool is a factory, and that factory is usually a singleton. Flyweights are often the leaves of a Composite: the tree holds the positions, and the shared leaf holds the appearance.

Proxy

stand-in

Put a stand-in in front of an object to control how it is reached.

What it is

The proxy has the same interface as the real object, and holds it or knows how to find it. Callers cannot tell the difference. So the proxy is free to delay creating it, check permissions, cache answers, or send the call over the network — before passing it on, or instead of passing it on.

Why it exists

The alternative is putting lazy loading, security checks, retries and logging inside the real class, where they do not belong and cannot be changed per environment. A proxy keeps those rules outside and leaves calling code untouched. The costs are one more layer and a few well-known traps. You cannot tell whether an object is a proxy, equals and identity get tricky, and a call from one method to another inside the same object skips the proxy completely. That last one is the most common reason an annotation seems not to work.

Use it when

  • The real object is expensive to build and may never be used.
  • The real object lives in another process or on another machine.
  • Who may use it depends on who is calling.
  • You need to count, log, cache or lock around every use.

Typical use cases

  • ORM lazy loading for links that may never be followed.
  • Transactions, caching and retries added by a framework.
  • RPC stubs that turn a local call into a network call.
  • A guard that checks tenant or role before letting a call through.

Think twice when

  • The extra behaviour is something the caller chose to add. That is a Decorator.
  • Callers need the real type, reference identity, or a final class.

Don't confuse it with

Same shape as Decorator, different purpose. A decorator adds behaviour the caller chose. A proxy controls access to something the caller thinks it already has. A remote proxy on one side plus an Adapter on the other is what most RPC clients look like.

Chain of Responsibility

pass it along

Pass a request along a line of handlers until one of them deals with it.

What it is

Handlers are linked one after another. Each one either handles the request, passes it to the next, or stops it there. The sender knows only the first handler and never finds out who answered.

Why it exists

Otherwise the sender needs an if for every possible handler, and that if has to change whenever the policy changes. A chain lets you add, remove or reorder steps when you wire it up, and each handler has one job. The risks: a request can reach the end with nobody handling it, the order becomes knowledge nobody wrote down, and debugging means walking the whole chain.

Use it when

  • Several objects could handle a request and you do not know which one in advance.
  • The handlers, or their order, should be configurable.
  • You want a pipeline where any step may stop the rest.

Typical use cases

  • HTTP middleware: login check, rate limit, validation, logging.
  • Approval flows where the amount decides who signs off.
  • Error handlers that pass a problem further up.
  • Support tickets moving through levels.

Think twice when

  • One handler is always the right one. Call it directly.
  • A request that quietly goes unhandled would be a bug, and nothing forces a last handler.
  • The order matters and nobody has written it down.

Don't confuse it with

Decorator also stacks wrappers, but a decorator always passes the call on and only adds behaviour. A handler in a chain can swallow the request. Command is often the request object that travels down the chain.

Command

call as object

Turn a request into an object, so you can store it, queue it, log it and undo it.

What it is

A command object holds the target and everything the action needs, and has an execute() method — often with a matching undo(). Whatever triggers it, such as a button, an endpoint or a scheduler, knows nothing about what it does or to whom.

Why it exists

A method call happens and is gone. You cannot save it, delay it, retry it, or reverse it. As an object it has a life, so the same action can run now, wait in a queue, be written to a log, be replayed after a crash, or be rolled back. That is the basis of undo stacks, job queues and event sourcing. The overhead is one class per action, so simple actions are better left as lambdas.

Use it when

  • You need undo, redo, or a record of actions you can replay.
  • Actions must be queued, scheduled, retried, or run on another thread.
  • The trigger and the logic must be separate, so several buttons can run the same action.
  • You want a record of what was asked for, not only of what changed.

Typical use cases

  • Undo and redo in an editor.
  • Background job and task queues.
  • Bigger actions made by grouping smaller ones.
  • CQRS, where each command is a message with its own handler.

Think twice when

  • A one-line action with no undo, queue or log.
  • Commands start calling each other and quietly become a second model.

Don't confuse it with

Command with Memento gives you undo when an action cannot be reversed by simple arithmetic. Command with Composite gives you macro commands. Command with Chain of Responsibility is what most middleware looks like.

Interpreter

tiny language

Turn a small language into classes, and run a sentence by walking those objects.

What it is

Each rule of the grammar becomes a class. Simple rules produce a value directly. Bigger rules hold smaller ones and combine their results. A sentence becomes a tree of objects, which you run against a context that supplies the variables.

Why it exists

When rules have to be edited by non-programmers, or changed without a release, writing them in Java is the wrong shape. As objects, each rule can be tested alone and combined at runtime. It only stays manageable for small grammars: the number of classes grows with the grammar, so anything with real syntax deserves a parser tool such as ANTLR instead.

Use it when

  • The language is small and unlikely to grow much.
  • Rules must be combined or changed while the program runs.
  • The same tree must support several operations, such as run, explain and optimise.

Typical use cases

  • Feature-flag rules such as country == IN AND tier == gold.
  • Search or filter expressions built from a query string.
  • Pricing and discount rules.
  • Small validation languages and formula evaluators.

Think twice when

  • The grammar is big. Use a parser generator.
  • Speed matters. Walking a tree is slow next to compiled code.

Don't confuse it with

The tree itself is a Composite. A parser usually builds it, playing the role of a Builder. New operations over it are normally added with a Visitor, rather than more methods on every node.

Iterator

traverse

Go through the items of a collection one by one, without showing how they are stored.

What it is

The position moves out of the collection and into a separate object with hasNext() and next(). The collection keeps the data; the iterator keeps the place. Several iterators can walk the same collection at the same time.

Why it exists

If the collection walks itself, only one walk can happen at a time, and every caller learns how it is built inside. Moving that out means an array, a tree, a ring buffer and a paged API can all be read by identical code. It also allows laziness: fetch the next page only when someone actually asks for it. The cost is one small object per walk, plus the question of what happens if the data changes while you are walking.

Use it when

  • Callers must go through the items without knowing the internal layout.
  • You need several orders, or several walks at the same time.
  • The sequence is lazy, remote, paged, or endless.

Typical use cases

  • Every for-each loop in Java.
  • Walking a tree in different orders behind one interface.
  • Reading a paged API or a database cursor as one flat sequence.
  • Reading a file line by line instead of loading all of it.

Think twice when

  • Java already gives you one. Implement Iterable instead of inventing a second API.
  • Callers genuinely need to jump to an item by index.

Don't confuse it with

Iterator is the usual way to walk a Composite, and Visitor is the usual way to do something new to one. An iterator hands items out; a visitor pushes an operation in.

Mediator

hub, not mesh

Put the rules for how a group of objects talk in one place, so they stop calling each other.

What it is

Each object holds the mediator instead of holding its neighbours. When something happens, it tells the mediator, and the mediator decides who else needs to react. The rules for the group live in one class instead of being spread across all of them.

Why it exists

If several objects all know each other, the number of links grows very fast, and none of them can be reused or tested on its own. Sending everything through one hub cuts the links down to one per object, and puts the whole protocol in a single class you can read. The obvious risk is that the hub collects every rule in the system and becomes a god object. It is the same trade-off as orchestration versus choreography in distributed systems.

Use it when

  • Many objects talk to many other objects.
  • A component cannot be reused because it is tangled up with its neighbours.
  • The rules between components change more often than the components do.

Typical use cases

  • A form where enabling one field depends on several others.
  • Chat rooms passing messages between people.
  • Air-traffic-control style coordination of independent parts.
  • A service that runs a multi-step business process end to end.

Think twice when

  • Only two objects talk to each other. Let them.
  • The mediator has grown past a few hundred lines of routing rules.

Don't confuse it with

Mediator and Observer get mixed up often. Observer is a broadcast: the subject does not know or care who is listening. Mediator is a coordinator: it knows every part and drives them. Real mediators often use Observer inside to receive the events.

Memento

snapshot

Save an object's state so it can be restored later, without showing that state to anyone else.

What it is

There are three roles. The owner makes a snapshot of its own state and can load one back. The snapshot is a closed box. The keeper stores snapshots and hands them back, but never looks inside. Only the owner can read the contents, so nothing is exposed.

Why it exists

Undo, rollback and checkpoints all need a copy of “before”. The easy way is to add getters and setters for every field, so an outside class can save and restore them. That breaks encapsulation and freezes the internal layout into your public API. A memento keeps that knowledge inside the class that owns it. The costs are memory, since snapshots of big objects add up, and versioning if the snapshots are ever written to disk.

Use it when

  • You need undo, rollback, or checkpoints.
  • The snapshot must not force the object to expose its fields.
  • An operation might have to be abandoned and reversed.

Typical use cases

  • Undo stacks in editors and drawing tools.
  • Save points in a long form or wizard.
  • Game saves.
  • Undoing changes to an object after a failed check.

Think twice when

  • The state is huge and snapshots would fill memory. Store the difference, or a reverse Command.
  • The object holds things that cannot be snapshotted.

Don't confuse it with

Memento supplies the state that Command's undo() needs when an action cannot be reversed by simple arithmetic. Prototype often provides the copying that makes the snapshot.

Observer

notify many

When one object changes, tell everything that depends on it, automatically.

What it is

The subject keeps a list of listeners, and lets them join and leave. When its state changes, it calls one known method on each listener. It knows the listener interface and nothing else: not the real types, not how many there are, not what they do.

Why it exists

If the reactions are written where the change happens, that class must be edited every time a new reaction is added, and it slowly collects dependencies on unrelated parts of the system. Observer turns this around: the reactions sign themselves up. The costs are worth saying out loud in an interview. The order of the calls is not defined. A slow or failing listener hurts the publisher. Listeners that never leave the list cause memory leaks. And the flow becomes hard to follow in a debugger.

Use it when

  • One change must trigger reactions that the changing object should not know about.
  • Who is interested changes while the program runs.
  • You are modelling domain events, or a UI that redraws on change.

Typical use cases

  • Publishing an event when an order is placed, picked up by billing, analytics and email.
  • UI widgets redrawing when their model changes.
  • Clearing a cache when something is written.
  • Reactive streams, which are Observer plus flow control.

Think twice when

  • The reaction is required and must happen in a fixed order. A direct call is clearer and easier to test.
  • Listeners are slow and the publisher is on a fast path. Publish in the background instead.
  • Nobody owns the listener's lifetime, so it will outlive its usefulness.

Don't confuse it with

Observer broadcasts without knowing who listens. Mediator knows every part by name and drives them. Publish–subscribe puts a broker in the middle, so neither side holds a reference to the other — which is what lets it work across separate processes.

State

behaviour by state

Let an object behave differently as its state changes, as if it had changed class.

What it is

Each state becomes a class with the same interface, and the object passes its work to whichever state it is in. To change state, you swap that object. A status field surrounded by if statements becomes a few small classes with clear moves between them.

Why it exists

Behaviour based on a status field usually means the same switch copied into every method, and every new status means checking all of them. That is how “we forgot to handle CANCELLED in refund()” happens. With one class per state, an illegal action becomes impossible instead of merely unlikely, and the state machine can be read. The cost is more classes, and moves scattered across states unless you deliberately keep them in one place.

Use it when

  • Behaviour depends on a status, and the same big if appears in several methods.
  • The moves between states are well defined and worth writing down.
  • Doing the wrong thing in a given state should fail loudly, in one place.

Typical use cases

  • Order life cycle: draft, paid, packed, shipped, delivered, cancelled.
  • Connection and session life cycles.
  • Document approval flows.
  • Vending machines, media players and protocol handlers.

Think twice when

  • There are two states and one if.
  • The state is stored in a database and you have no clean mapping from status to class.

Don't confuse it with

State and Strategy have exactly the same drawing, which is why they get asked about together. In Strategy the caller picks the algorithm, and the algorithms know nothing about each other. In State the object picks its own next state, so states know what comes next and the caller just calls methods.

Strategy

swap algorithm

Put each version of an algorithm in its own class, and swap them while the program runs.

What it is

The part that varies moves out of the class and behind an interface. The class holds one of those objects and calls it. So the behaviour is chosen by whoever wires the object up, instead of by an if inside it.

Why it exists

An if that picks behaviour has to be edited for every new option, and the data each option needs ends up on a class that only sometimes uses it. With Strategy each version can be tested, measured and released on its own, and the choice can come from configuration, a feature flag, or the request itself. The costs: the caller has to know the options exist, and you get more types — which lambdas and method references make cheap in Java.

Use it when

  • There are several ways to do one thing, and the choice varies by input, customer or config.
  • A method has an if whose branches are all versions of the same operation.
  • The versions need to be tested or rolled out separately.

Typical use cases

  • Shipping cost, pricing, discount and tax calculations.
  • Compression, serialization or encryption chosen per request.
  • Retry and backoff policies.
  • Sorting and matching rules supplied by the caller.

Think twice when

  • There is one algorithm and no likely second one.
  • The branches are not really interchangeable and need different inputs.

Don't confuse it with

Strategy uses composition. Template Method solves the same problem with inheritance, by fixing the steps in a parent class. State looks identical but its objects change themselves, instead of being chosen by the caller.

Template Method

fixed skeleton

Fix the order of steps in a parent class, and let subclasses fill in some of the steps.

What it is

A method in the parent class runs the steps in a fixed order and calls methods that subclasses provide. The order and the work around it cannot be changed, so that method should be final.

Why it exists

Different versions of a process usually share the same flow — open, check, process, commit, close — and differ only in the middle. Copying that flow into each version means fixing every bug several times, and any version can quietly skip the commit. Keeping the flow in one place makes that impossible. The cost is inheritance: a class can extend only one parent, subclasses depend on the parent's internals, and versions cannot be mixed. Where that hurts, Strategy is the alternative.

Use it when

  • Several versions share the same sequence and differ in only a few steps.
  • The order itself is a rule worth enforcing.
  • You are writing a framework and want clear places for users to plug in.

Typical use cases

  • Batch jobs: read, check, transform, write, report.
  • Test life cycles with fixed setup and teardown around your code.
  • A base controller or servlet that handles requests in a fixed way.
  • Startup sequences with steps you can override.

Think twice when

  • Versions need to be mixed and matched. That needs composition.
  • Subclasses override steps to get around the flow instead of filling it in.
  • You would need to extend two parents.

Don't confuse it with

Template Method and Strategy solve the same problem in opposite ways: inheritance or composition. Framework classes like JdbcTemplate mix the two — a fixed sequence, with the changing step passed in as an object rather than written in a subclass.

Visitor

new operations

Add new operations to a set of classes without changing those classes.

What it is

Each class gets an accept(Visitor) method that calls back the visitor method matching its own type. That two-step call is how the right operation is chosen for the right type. A new operation is then a new visitor class, and the original classes never change.

Why it exists

When one stable set of classes needs many unrelated operations — run, print, check, export — putting them all on the classes turns each one into a mixed bag. Visitor keeps one operation in one class, where all its cases sit together. The catch is the opposite problem: adding an operation is cheap, but adding a new class breaks every visitor you have. Use it only when the classes are more stable than the operations.

Use it when

  • The set of classes is stable, but new operations keep arriving.
  • The operations have nothing to do with the classes' real job and would clutter them.
  • An operation needs to collect results while walking a mixed structure.

Typical use cases

  • Compilers and linters walking a syntax tree.
  • Exporting one document model to several formats.
  • Code analysis tools.
  • Producing several different reports from one object tree.

Think twice when

  • New types are likely. Then every visitor becomes a place you must edit.
  • The structure is small and two methods on the classes would do.
  • In modern Java, sealed types with pattern matching say the same thing far more simply.

Don't confuse it with

Visitor is almost always used on a Composite or on an Interpreter's tree, and it often uses an Iterator to reach every node.

Telling look-alikes apart

Patterns that get mixed up usually share the same drawing and differ only in purpose. These are the four comparisons that come up most often.

Adapter vs Decorator vs Proxy vs Facade

Adapter
Changes one interface into a different one your code already expects. Added later, around something you cannot change.
Decorator
Keeps the interface and adds behaviour. You choose it, you can stack several, and it always passes the call on.
Proxy
Keeps the interface and controls access: later, remotely, or only if you are allowed. Usually made by the framework, not by you.
Facade
Adds a new, smaller interface in front of many classes. It simplifies; it does not replace.

Strategy vs State vs Template Method

Strategy
You pick the algorithm. The algorithms know nothing about each other. Built with a field.
State
The object picks its own next state, so each state knows what comes next. Same drawing, opposite direction of control.
Template Method
The steps are fixed in a parent class and subclasses fill in the gaps. Built with inheritance instead of a field.

Observer vs Mediator vs Pub–Sub

Observer
One subject, many listeners, one direction. The subject does not know who is listening.
Mediator
One hub that knows every part and tells them what to do. Messages travel both ways.
Pub–Sub
A broker sits in the middle, so neither side holds the other. That is what lets it work across separate processes.

Factory Method vs Abstract Factory vs Builder

Factory Method
One product, chosen by a subclass that overrides the creating method.
Abstract Factory
A group of products that must match. You swap the whole factory to change the group.
Builder
One complicated product, put together step by step and checked once at the end.

Beyond the original 23

The original book came before distributed systems, DI containers and lambdas. These patterns matter just as much in a modern service, and a senior interview is more likely to ask about them.

Dependency Injection
Objects are handed what they need from outside, instead of creating it themselves. This is why modern Java needs Singleton and Factory far less than the 1994 book suggests.
Repository
Storage behind an interface that looks like a collection, so business code never sees queries or the ORM.
Unit of Work
Keeps track of everything changed during one business action, then saves it all together.
Null Object
An object that does nothing, so callers stop writing null checks.
Object Pool
Reuses objects that are slow to create, such as connections, threads and buffers, instead of making new ones.
Specification
Business rules written as small objects you can test, combine, and also turn into database queries.
Circuit Breaker
Stops calling a failing service for a while, so one slow service does not use up the threads of everyone calling it.
Saga
A long business process split into local steps, each with an undo step, because one shared transaction across services is not practical.
CQRS
Separate models for writing and for reading, so each can be tuned and scaled on its own.
Outbox
Save the event in the same database transaction as the change, then publish it. The standard fix for writing to two places and having one fail.

Using this in an interview

These questions are rarely about remembering names. They are about whether you can say what problem made the extra layer worth it, and what it cost you.

“Which pattern would you use here?”

Describe the problem before you name the pattern: what changes, what must stay the same, and what breaks today. Naming a pattern without the problem sounds memorised. Describing the problem without naming a pattern still sounds senior.

“Why not just use an if statement?”

Because that if has to be edited for every new case, in every method that checks the same field. That is the Open–Closed argument, and it is the honest answer for Strategy, State and Factory alike.

“Where have you actually used one?”

Pick a real one. Say what the problem was, what you changed, and what it cost you. Admitting the cost is what separates a real answer from a textbook one.

“Isn't this over-engineering?”

Often it is. A good line to hold: a pattern earns its extra layer when a second version already exists or is planned. Not when you can merely imagine one.

“What has changed since the GoF book?”

Lambdas shrink Strategy and Command to one line. DI containers take over Singleton and Factory. Sealed types with pattern matching replace most Visitors. Records make Prototype and Memento almost free. The ideas survive; most of the extra code does not.

“Aggregation or composition here?”

Answer with the delete question, not the diamond. If deleting the whole must delete the part, and nothing else may hold it, that is composition. Otherwise it is aggregation. In Java the difference shows up in who calls new, and whether the list is ever handed out.

“Which SOLID principle does this violate?”

Name the symptom before the letter. A growing switch is Open–Closed. A subclass that throws on an inherited method is Liskov, usually caused by an interface that was too big. A business class importing a driver package is Dependency Inversion. A class three teams keep editing is Single Responsibility.

“Which patterns does the framework already give you?”

Pointing at Proxy in @Transactional, Template Method in JdbcTemplate, Chain of Responsibility in a filter chain, and Observer in the event publisher shows you have read the framework, not only its documentation.
